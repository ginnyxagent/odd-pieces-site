01 — Who We Are

The Brand Story

Odd Pieces turns a puzzle into a story with a twist.

We make 1000-piece jigsaw puzzles for people who want surprise, not scenery.

Every Odd Pieces puzzle has three beats: the box is the before, the puzzle is the after, and the Secret Ending Envelope explains what the hell happened.

The goal is simple: make someone say WAIT, WHAT?!, call everyone over, and point at the same tiny detail like it just personally offended them.

"A puzzle for people who think regular puzzles are a waste of a perfectly good table."

03 — Who It's For

Our Audience

Odd Pieces has four core audiences: the thoughtful gift-giver, the real puzzle fan, the returning loyalist, and the active retiree buying for actual leisure.

Audience

Giftable enough to buy.
Good enough to keep.

Most first purchases come from gifters. The strongest repeat behavior comes from puzzlers and loyalists.

What they share: they want something clever, specific, and not interchangeable with a generic puzzle.

That is exactly why it works as both a gift and a self-purchase.

If you can handle a plot twist, you're exactly who this is for.

The Golden Rule

If another puzzle brand could say it, rewrite it.

✓ Sound Like This

Shocking before it's cute
Fun before it's refined
Playful before it's serious
Irreverent with control
Direct, punchy, no filler words
Calls the reader in, doesn't talk down
Humor that comes from being genuinely clever
Urgency without desperation

✗ Never Sound Like This

"For the whole family!" (too Walmart, too safe)
"An immersive puzzle experience" (dry corporate, immediately skip)
"The perfect gift this holiday season" (zzz)
"Hours of entertainment" (box copy from 1992)
"Premium quality guaranteed" (says literally nothing)
"Recommended for ages 18+" (we don't age-gate — everyone's welcome)
Luxury editorial tone (too stiff, wrong vibe)
Corporate / dry / formal (if a PR department wrote it, delete it)

Voice Principles

Practical test: write the clear version first. Then sharpen it. Keep the point. Cut the fluff.

Shock First, Explain Later

Lead with the surprising, weird, funny thing. Let curiosity do the work. Don't front-load context.

Confident, Not Arrogant

We know we're different. We don't need to yell about it. Confidence is quiet. Arrogance is loud.

Play It Straight

The best humor is delivered deadpan. Don't wink too hard. Say the absurd thing like it's obvious.

Usage Combinations

Lead by example

Blue and yellow should visibly share the stage. If the guidelines look mostly dark, the system will be misread as mostly dark.

Balance Rule

Across any major brand system, campaign, or web page, blue should show up as a true hero area, not just a soft supporting tint. If yellow gets the spotlight, blue gets a wall.

Yellow + Ink

Primary combo. Hero moments.

Blue + Ink

Background sections. Secondary UI.

Ink + Yellow

Dark sections. High contrast.

Color Rule Yellow and blue are both dominant colors — not yellow hero, blue secondary. Both should appear heavily throughout any design. The website uses blue as a primary background across entire sections. Follow that.

08 — What Makes The Art Hit

More To Notice Than Feels Fair

The art should reward people who look once, then twice, then stop the person next to them so they can point at something stupid in the background. Density matters. Payoff matters. The joke cannot be one-and-done.

The standard

Every time you look back, there should be one more thing to notice.

That might be a visual joke, a character payoff, a background gag, a reference, or a clue that only becomes funny once you understand the whole scene.

01

Comedic payoffs

The best scenes do not just look busy. They pay off. One object causes another problem. One background detail makes the foreground joke funnier. The scene should keep cashing checks.

02

Pop culture energy

References can show up, but they should feel remixed, hidden, sideways, or cleverly disguised. Never lazy parody. Never “look, an IP.”

03

Metauniverse threads

Characters, objects, logos, symbols, and tiny weird details should echo across titles. The universe should feel connected without turning into homework.

04

Maximum density

If there is empty space, it should be a choice, not an accident. The art should feel packed with things worth discovering, not decorated with filler.

Quick gut check

Does the scene reward close looking?
Is there more than one joke, reveal, or payoff?
Do the references feel clever instead of obvious?
Would someone point at a tiny detail and make everyone else come look?
