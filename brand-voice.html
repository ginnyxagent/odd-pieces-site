Brand context

Rules

Voice rules

The golden rule

If you could swap our copy into another puzzle brand’s email and it would still work, rewrite it.

Core principle

Be clever and clear. Intrigue people, but never make them wonder what the email is actually about.

Subject lines

Subject line formulas

The tease

  • Something odd this way comes (hint: 1,000 pieces)
  • You weren’t supposed to see this yet, new puzzle, early access
  • Open if you dare. We just dropped something strange.
  • A mystery puzzle so weird we almost didn’t release it

The challenge

  • Think you’re clever enough for our hardest puzzle yet?
  • Only 2% solve this on the first try. New drop inside.
  • This one broke our testers. Your turn.
  • We dare you to finish this in one sitting

The oddball

  • We put a secret in box #4,217. Yours might be next.
  • Our designer lost sleep over this puzzle (worth it)
  • This email contains zero normal puzzles
  • We asked 50 puzzlers what’s odd. Their answers surprised us.

The insider

  • Puzzlers only: first look at our newest mystery
  • For our weirdest fans, early access inside
  • You’re on the list. Here’s what’s coming.
  • VIP puzzler alert: 24 hours before everyone else

The straight shooter

  • New puzzle just dropped. It’s a weird one.
  • 20% off everything. No riddle, just savings.
  • Your order shipped, brace yourself
  • Back in stock: the one that sold out in 3 hours

CTAs

CTA guidelines

The formula: action word that’s on-brand + what they’re getting.

Product launch

  • See the new puzzle
  • Explore the collection
  • Snag it before it’s gone
  • Get the details
  • Grab yours, limited run

Promo & sale

  • Save 20%, oddly good deal
  • Unlock your discount
  • Treat yourself (you solved that last one fast)
  • Browse the sale, ends Friday

Win-back & re-engagement

  • See what you’ve been missing
  • Pick up where you left off
  • Your next puzzle is waiting
  • Come back, we made something new

Full email examples

Example 1

New product launch

Subject: Something strange just arrived (1,000 pieces of it)
Preview: Our oddest puzzle yet. Naturally.
Header: You’ve never solved anything like this.
Body: We spent 6 months designing a puzzle that would make you question everything. The image? Unexpected. The difficulty? Let’s just say our testers needed snacks.
It’s 1,000 pieces. It’s limited edition. And it’s the kind of thing you’ll want to frame when you’re done (if you finish).
CTA: See the new puzzle
P.S. We hid something in the box. You’ll know it when you find it.
Example 2

Promo / sale

Subject: 25% off. No riddle. Just savings.
Preview: Our biggest sale of the year, for real.
Header: We’ll keep this simple (for once).
Body: Everything on the site is 25% off through Sunday. Mystery puzzles, classics, gift bundles, all of it.
No catch. No secret code. No puzzle to solve first. (We know. Odd.)
CTA: Browse the sale, ends Sunday
P.S. Okay fine, there IS a secret code: ODDBALL25. But you didn’t need it. Everything’s already discounted. We just like secrets.
Example 3

Brand story / newsletter

Subject: The puzzle that almost didn’t happen
Preview: A behind-the-scenes story from the Odd Pieces studio.
Header: Some puzzles come easy. This one fought us.
Body: Three redesigns. One heated debate about a single shade of blue. And a prototype that our founder’s cat destroyed.
But that’s how the best Odd Pieces puzzles happen, through stubbornness, weirdness, and a refusal to make anything normal.
Here’s the story of how [Puzzle Name] went from napkin sketch to your dining table.
CTA: Read the full story
P.S. The cat is fine. The prototype was not.
Example 4

Win-back

Subject: It’s been a while. We made something new.
Preview: 3 new puzzles since you last visited.
Header: Still there? Good.
Body: We get it. Life happens. Tables get used for dinner instead of puzzles. But while you were away, we’ve been busy being odd.
Since your last visit: 3 new mystery puzzles, a collab we can’t stop talking about, and a 500-piece puzzle that’s harder than some 1,000-piece ones.
CTA: See what’s new
P.S. Your dining table misses you. (For puzzle reasons.)
Example 5

Transactional

Subject: Your order is confirmed, the mystery begins
Preview: Order #12345 is on its way to being packed.
Header: Nice choice. Seriously.
Body: We’ve got your order and we’re packing it up now. Here’s what’s coming your way:
[Order details]
Estimated delivery: [date]. We’ll send you tracking as soon as it ships.
CTA: Track your order
P.S. Pro tip: clear the table now. You’ll thank us later.
Example 6

Gift / holiday

Subject: The gift that confuses people (in the best way)
Preview: Mystery puzzles make oddly perfect gifts.
Header: Give the gift of “what the heck is this?”
Body: Normal gifts get a polite thank-you. Odd Pieces puzzles get a double-take, a confused smile, and then hours of obsession.
Whether it’s for the person who has everything or the person who loves a good challenge, we’ve got something that’ll land.
CTA: Shop gift-worthy puzzles
P.S. We gift wrap. And yes, the wrapping paper is also odd.

Style

Writing style quick rules

  • Sentence case, not Title Case. Keep it casual, not corporate.
  • Short paragraphs. 2 to 3 sentences max per block.
  • Use “you” more than “we.” The reader is the hero.
  • One CTA per email. Pick one action and make it irresistible.
  • CTAs tell them what they’re getting. Clever but clear wins.
  • Read it out loud. If it sounds like a robot or a boardroom, rewrite.
  • Hide an easter egg. A weird detail, small joke, or fun P.S. goes a long way.
  • Always include a P.S. It’s the most-read part of any email.
  • Don’t be cryptic. Intriguing is good. Confusing is not.

Before sending

Pre-send checklist

  • Would this email make sense coming from any other brand?
  • Is the subject line curiosity-driven and clear enough to open?
  • Does the opening line hook within 5 words?
  • Is there a twist, surprise, or odd detail somewhere?
  • Is the CTA clear about what happens when they click?
  • Does the CTA still sound like Odd Pieces?
  • Would I actually want to read this if it landed in my inbox?
  • Is the P.S. doing something fun?
  • Did I read it out loud and does it sound human?
  • Is it short enough?
Remember Normal is boring. Odd is the point. But odd doesn’t mean unclear.
